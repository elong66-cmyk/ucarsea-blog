## The assumption that costs a dealer money

"It's electric, so it gets the EV rate." That sentence is right for a growing share of what leaves a Chinese dealer lot — and wrong often enough to matter. Range-extended EVs (EREVs) — the Li Auto L-series, the Aito M7/M9, several Voyah and Deepal trims — market themselves as electric, drive like electric most of the time, and still carry a **small gasoline engine on board whose only job is to spin a generator and recharge the battery**. That engine is the detail that decides which tax schedule applies, and in both Laos and Cambodia, the pure-EV incentive programs were written with battery-electric vehicles (BEVs) in mind — not hybrids of any kind.

This is not a rare edge case. EREVs are one of the fastest-growing categories in China's domestic used market precisely because they solve range anxiety without needing charging infrastructure. A dealer sourcing family SUVs is going to run into them constantly. Getting the tax classification wrong on a quote is how a competitive landed price turns into a margin-eating surprise at the port.

## What an EREV actually is, mechanically

Skip the marketing language and look at the drivetrain:

- **BEV (battery-electric vehicle):** battery + electric motor(s). No combustion engine, no fuel tank, no tailpipe. Full stop.
- **PHEV (plug-in hybrid):** battery + electric motor + combustion engine, where the combustion engine **can directly drive the wheels** in some conditions.
- **EREV (extended-range EV / "range extender"):** battery + electric motor + a small combustion engine — but the combustion engine's job is narrower than a PHEV's. It runs a generator to top up the battery; the wheels are still driven electrically. In daily use it can feel indistinguishable from a BEV. Under the hood, and on the customs form, it is not one.

The Li Auto L9 — today's featured unit, and the model that triggered this note — is a textbook EREV: a 1.5T turbo engine that never directly turns the wheels, paired with a battery pack good for roughly 180km of pure-electric range before the range extender kicks in. It has a fuel tank. It has a combustion engine listed on the VIN plate and the registration certificate. That is enough for most customs schedules to classify it as something other than a BEV.

> **Methodology note.** The Laos and Cambodia figures elsewhere on this blog come from published ministry notices for battery-electric vehicles specifically. Neither government has, to our knowledge, published an EREV-specific customs bulletin — the read here follows the general customs principle that classification is driven by drivetrain composition (presence of an ICE) rather than marketing category, which is standard practice across the region's hybrid/EV duty schedules. Treat the EREV tax gap described below as the likely default treatment, and confirm the specific HS classification with your clearing agent before finalizing a quote on any given unit.

## Laos: the exemption is written for BEVs, not "electric-branded" vehicles

We've covered the Laos EV framework in detail — the fuel-vehicle import suspension, the 0% import duty on EVs, and the **excise-tax exemption for vehicles under $50,000** (see our [Laos fuel ban / EV opportunity report](/blog/laos-fuel-import-ban-china-ev-opportunity-2026/)). The detail that matters here: that framework is built around **battery-electric vehicles**. A vehicle with an internal combustion engine on board — even one that never drives the wheels — sits in a different bucket.

Practically, this means:

- **A BEV under $50,000** clears with the full stack: 0% import duty, 0% excise (exemption), 10% VAT.
- **An EREV**, because it carries a combustion engine, is typically assessed closer to a **hybrid or conventional vehicle schedule** — which in Laos means **import duty and excise apply at standard (non-exempt) rates**, not the EV concession.

The gap is not trivial. On a $42,000 unit, losing the excise exemption alone can add several thousand dollars to the landed cost versus what a dealer would pay assuming "electric = exempt." If you quoted a Lao buyer using BEV-rate assumptions on an EREV, you are underwater the moment the actual assessment comes back.

## Cambodia: the same trap, different mechanism

Cambodia's EV duty break — covered in our [Cambodia EV zero-duty guide](/blog/cambodia-ev-zero-import-duty-dealer-guide-2026/) — likewise targets vehicles that run **purely on electric power**. Cambodian customs practice has generally treated range-extended and plug-in hybrid vehicles under the **hybrid vehicle category**, which carries its own (more moderate, but non-zero) duty treatment rather than the full EV exemption. The practical result mirrors Laos: an EREV is not automatically waved through at BEV rates just because the spec sheet says "electric range" first.

## How to tell what you're sourcing before you quote

For any unit that markets itself as electric, check three things on the Chinese-side documentation before building a landed-cost quote for Laos or Cambodia:

1. **Powertrain type field on the vehicle registration / 行驶证.** It will say one of: 纯电动 (pure electric / BEV), 插电式混合动力 (plug-in hybrid / PHEV), or 增程式电动 (range-extended / EREV). This is the single fastest check.
2. **Does the vehicle have a fuel tank and an ICE listed?** If yes, it is not a BEV, regardless of how the model is marketed.
3. **Manufacturer's own classification.** Li Auto, Aito, Voyah, and Deepal all publish EREV/PHEV specs clearly in their configuration sheets — the information is available, it just has to be checked before the quote goes out, not after clearance.

We flag the powertrain type explicitly on every EV/EREV/PHEV listing we send — it's on the inspection report, not just the marketing description — precisely because this distinction changes the landed-cost math by thousands of dollars.

## What this means for a car like the L9

None of this makes the Li Auto L9 a bad sourcing choice — it's a genuinely strong unit: six-seat flagship packaging, ~180km EV range for daily driving with zero range anxiety on longer routes, and a China benchmark price of $51,200 against our EXW of $42,077 (a real 17.8% gap, not an inflated one). The point is not "avoid EREVs." The point is **quote them on the correct tax schedule from the start** so the landed number you give a Lao or Cambodian dealer is the number that survives customs, not a number that assumed BEV treatment by mistake.

## The playbook

- **Classify before you quote.** Check 纯电动 vs 插电式混合动力 vs 增程式电动 on the registration document for every "electric" listing.
- **Model two landed-cost scenarios when in doubt** — BEV-rate and hybrid-rate — and quote the conservative one until you've confirmed local customs treatment for that specific HS classification.
- **Don't let marketing language set your tax assumption.** "Electric" in a listing title tells you the drivetrain feel, not the customs category.
- **Flag it to the buyer up front.** A dealer who knows a unit is an EREV and prices accordingly closes faster than one who finds out at the port.
