## Why the bottom shelf works

- **Depreciation did the work.** A K3 or Polo has already burned off most of its value in China's brutally competitive domestic market. You are buying the flat part of the curve.
- **Parts are everywhere.** Kia and VW mechanical stock is global; no buyer in Vientiane or Accra worries about a filter.
- **First-car and fleet demand never sleeps.** Ride-hail drivers, driving schools, young families — the under-$10K landed segment is the deepest pool of buyers in every developing market we ship to.

## Where the math stops working

Honesty section. At this price point, **not everything cheap is a deal**:

- Units priced far *below* the domestic curve fail our fraud screen more often than they pass it — too cheap means flood, fire or accident history until proven otherwise.
- Some nameplates that look cheap EXW carry benchmark prices in Japan that are even cheaper. Not every model wins the comparison, which is why we publish sample counts (the *n=* figures above) instead of hand-waving.
- Freight is regressive: $1,200 of ocean on a $5,500 car is 22% of unit cost. Budget sedans want full containers, not single-unit shipments.

## Dealer playbook

1. **Buy in pairs or fours** — fill the container, halve the per-unit freight.
2. **Stay mainstream** — K3, Polo, Lavida-class VWs, entry Toyotas. Exotic badges at this price are parts orphans.
3. **Demand the inspection pack anyway.** A $5,800 car deserves the same structural report as a $58,000 one; skipping it is how cheap becomes expensive.

The under-$7K shelf is not glamorous, but it is the fastest-turning inventory in the business — and in 2026 the China lane prices it under the auction pipeline on the models that matter.
